Hafez Music Ontology

Years ago, I noticed Hafez (14th-century), one of the most distinguished and canonical poets in Persian literature, never used the word music in his poems (or rather its Persian equivalent, mūsīqī موسیقی). This is despite the fact that the term appears in the writings of his contemporaries and earlier poets, such as Saadi Shirazi (13th century) and Attar of Nishapur (12th century), as well as in scholarly works on music dating back to at least the 10th century. Among these is al-Fārābī’s major treatise al-Mūsīqā al-Kabīr (الموسيقى الكبير), or The Great Book of Music, written in Arabic. And yet, Divan of Hafez, his main book and one of the main books in Iranian cultural life, is full of references to musical moments, instruments, dance, and musical gestures.

The longstanding question on the ontology of music shifted, for me, from what music is to what music is for Hafez.

In this project, I have compiled all the couplets in Hafez’s poems that contain references to musical concepts, practices, instruments, or related objects. The search is based on the following terms:

مطرب (motreb, singer/musician/minstrel), طرب (tarab, musical ecstasy/joy), بربط (barbat, ancient Persian lute), پرده (parde, musical mode/fret/curtain), چنگ (chang, harp), رباب (robab, rabab), بانگ (bang, sound/call), آهنگ (ahang, melody/tune), صوت (sowt, sound/voice), ساز (saz, musical instrument), آواز (avaz, song/singing), نی (ney, reed flute), رود (rud, stringed instrument), دف (daf, frame drum), رقص (raqs, dance), سماع (sama, spiritual listening/whirling), دستان (dastan, musical fingering/fret), کمانچه (kamancheh, spike fiddle), نغمه (naghmeh, melody/tune), سرود (sorud, hymn/anthem/song).

These verses together reveal what that moment means to Hafez—the moment whose term remains unuttered throughout the book.

The first section presents a randomly selected couplet from Hafez’s poetry (from those related to music and dance). This draws on one of the ways the Divan has long existed in everyday Iranian life: as a book of divination, or fal-e Hafez: with both sacred and secular means, one opens the book at random and reads a ghazal, allowing its verses to offer an occasion for reflection, interpretation, or insight.

The second section provides an English translation of the selected couplet. These translations are generated by machine and remain limited. They are intended primarily to make the collection more accessible. The third section contains a searchable collection of all the identified couplets. Clicking on any verse shows its English translation.

How the couplets were chosen

Several of these words lead double lives: رود is a river, a stringed instrument, or the verb “goes”; پرده is a veil, a fret, or a musical mode; نی is a reed flute or “not”; ساز is an instrument or the verb “make”. Each candidate couplet was read in context, and those where the word carries only a non-musical sense were left out. A handful of borderline cases (mostly plain “joy”, طرب, or an ordinary voice) are held back as well. Matched words are highlighted in each couplet.

The Persian text comes from Ganjoor, and every couplet links back to its page there. The English renderings are working translations prepared with Claude: literal rather than poetic, and open to correction.
